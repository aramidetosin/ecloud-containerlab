Multi-DC anycast on a routed L3 backbone

A single virtual IP serves both DCs by anycast; users pick a region and consume the other DC's data east-west over a routed backbone; the firewall handles only north-south internet. Every figure here is measured live in the lab.

Traffic & paths

Three scopes, three rules: within a DC = VXLAN/EVPN (no firewall, no backbone); across DCs = the backbone, east-west (no firewall); to external = the firewall, north-south. Here is every path, hop by hop, with the real addresses.

0 · Within a DC — VXLAN/EVPN, symmetric IRB

no firewall, no backbone

Each DC is an independent leaf-spine VXLAN/EVPN fabric. Same-subnet traffic is bridged leaf-to-leaf (L2VNI, EVPN type-2 MAC routes); different-subnet traffic is routed with symmetric IRB over an L3VNI (50001 tenant-k8s / 50002 tenant-svc in DC1; 50101/50102 in DC2). The spine only forwards the encapsulated packet as plain IP — it never sees a tenant route or a pod IP. Neither the firewall nor the backbone is involved for same-tenant intra-DC traffic; the one exception is crossing tenants (k8s↔svc), which hairpins the firewall because the tenants are separate VRFs.

A · North-south — internet reaches an app (external)

through the firewall

The firewall destination-NATs 10.80.15.50 → 192.168.202.0 and enforces the internet-to-k8s policy, then routes to the VIP. Because the backbone steers the anycast VIP to DC1 (local-pref 200), a client normally lands in DC1; return traffic hairpins back through the same firewall. An MSS clamp at the internet edge (1500) keeps every north-south flow inside the firewall's A/A HA3 limit.

B · East-west — DC1 talks to DC2

over the backbone, no firewall

This is the exact live traceroute from a DC1 pod to a DC2 node. Each fabric hands the packet to its border, the border strips VXLAN and routes plain IP across the backbone at 10.201.0.0, and the far border re-encapsulates into DC2's L3VNI. The firewall never appears. The routed segment carries the full decapsulated frame, so cross-region runs at jumbo 9000 end-to-end (measured, 0% loss).

Why not through the firewall? East-west over the firewall would (a) hairpin every cross-region packet through the A/A pair and its 1500-byte HA3 limit, and (b) make the firewall a bandwidth bottleneck for DC-to-DC replication. The backbone keeps the firewall for what it is good at — security at the internet edge.

C · Anycast selection & failover

BGP steering

Both DCs advertise 192.168.202.0/32 into the fabric by Cilium BGP. The backbone and firewall learn four paths — two per DC. A route-map (ANYCAST-STEER) sets local-preference 200 on the DC1 paths, so:

  • Steady state — the VIP resolves to DC1 (primary); DC2 paths sit as hot backup.
  • DC1 fails / withdraws — the backbone drops the DC1 paths and forwards to DC2 within a BGP convergence; no address changes, no DNS.
  • DC1 returns — the local-pref-200 path reappears and preempts, pulling the VIP back to DC1 automatically.

Internally each DC always prefers its own local copy (a DC1 node hitting the VIP stays in DC1). Determinism is only forced at the core so the demo and failover are repeatable.

Alongside the global VIP there are two regional endpoints (the AWS global-vs-regional pattern) so you can reach a specific DC on purpose — same app, DC-pinned: 10.80.15.51 always lands in DC1, 10.80.15.52 always in DC2. Each is a per-DC LoadBalancer IP (192.168.202.1/.2) announced by only that DC, DNAT'd at the firewall.

D · Region picker & cross-region consume

enters N-Sconsumes E-W

One user action exercises both paths at once. You reach the app north-south (path A, lands in DC1). When you pick the DC2 region, the DC1 pod makes a server-side call east-west (path B) to DC2's NodePort 10.168.10.x:30080 over the backbone, and returns DC2's inventory dataset — measured at ~5–10 ms. Reverse (DC2 consuming DC1's orders) is identical. The app also runs a live traceroute so you can watch the backbone hops in the page.

How the anycast routing works

The VIP is found by BGP — the same /32 is advertised into the fabric from both DCs and the network picks one. No DNS, no load-balancer appliance. Every value below is read live from the running fabric.

1 · Advertisement — the VIP climbs out of each DC

2 · Selection — the backbone sees four paths, and is steered

PathviaAS-pathlocal-prefrole
1–2DC1 border-1/265114 65100 65111 65010200primary (ECMP within DC1)
3–4DC2 border-1/265213 65200 65211 65020100hot backup

Both DCs advertise equal-length but different AS-paths, so BGP won't ECMP across DCs (multipath needs an identical AS-path) — the default winner is a non-deterministic tie-break. The route-map ANYCAST-STEER sets local-preference 200 on the DC1 paths as they enter the backbone, so DC1 is the deterministic primary and DC2 waits as hot standby. (DC1's own two border paths, being identical, are ECMP'd together.)

3 & 4 · To the client, and failover

The backbone advertises its winner (DC1) onward to the firewall, whose route to 192.168.202.0 is next-hop 10.201.0.0 (the aggr, verified by FIB lookup). So a request runs client → 10.80.15.50 → FW DNAT → 192.168.202.0 → aggr → DC1. If DC1 withdraws (pods gone, link down, whole DC lost) the backbone drops the local-pref-200 paths, promotes the DC2 backup, and re-advertises to the firewall — traffic shifts to DC2 within one BGP convergence. When DC1 returns its lp-200 path reappears and preempts. The client keeps the same 10.80.15.50 throughout — no DNS change (this is drill S4).

5 · Nearest-DC, internally

A node inside DC1 hitting the VIP reaches its own leaf's local announcement first (shortest path) and stays in DC1; a DC2 node stays in DC2. So the VIP is genuinely nearest-DC within the fabric — the local-pref steering only pins the single external (via-firewall) ingress to a deterministic primary, which keeps the demo and failover repeatable.

6 · Regional endpoints are the opposite of anycast

dc1.apps.ecloud.lab (192.168.202.1) is advertised by only DC1, dc2.apps (192.168.202.2) by only DC2 — single-origin /32s, so each routes deterministically to its own DC. That's the "choose a specific region" path; the shared .202.0 VIP is the "nearest / primary" anycast path.

7 · Source-based georouting — one VIP, per-client DC

The firewall can pin specific clients to specific DCs on the same VIP with source-based DNAT: 10.80.15.50 is translated to a DC-pinned internal address by the client's source IP, ordered above the generic anycast rule (first-match). In this POC client-1 (10.80.15.103) → 192.168.202.1 → DC1 is pinned, so it always lands on DC1 regardless of the backbone steering mode. external-client-2 (10.80.15.100) is deliberately left unpinned, so it follows the anycast toggle instead — DC1 under deterministic, hashing across both DCs under ECMP. Verified: under ECMP client-2's requests split ~9:5 across DC1/DC2 while client-1 stays 8/8 on DC1; restored to deterministic, both return to DC1. That contrast — one client hard-pinned, one following the fabric — shows source-DNAT georouting and anycast steering side by side on a single ingress.

8 · Toggle — deterministic vs ECMP anycast

The backbone steering is a one-command toggle (anycast_mode.sh <deterministic|ecmp>, on both aggr switches). Deterministic (default) = DC1 primary via local-pref 200, DC2 hot backup — one best DC, clean failover. ECMP = drop the local-pref and add as-path multipath-relax, so the backbone installs all four paths and unpinned flows hash across both DCs by 5-tuple (measured 12 / 8 across 20 requests from one client). Source-pinned clients stay deterministic in either mode, and failover works in both. ECMP is true load-shared anycast; deterministic is predictable-primary — pick per what you're demonstrating. Drive it from the CLI (anycast_mode.sh <mode> on eve-office) or from the operator console (below) — a UI on the client desktop that reads the live mode and flips it with a button.

The anycast controller — a host decides, the fabric forwards

The anycast decision has moved OFF the network devices onto a pair of GoBGP controller nodes. The old ANYCAST-STEER route-map is retired; two host controllers decide which DC serves 10.80.15.50 from live parameters.

Each controller (gobgp-1 active, gobgp-2 standby) runs GoBGP plus a policy brain. The brain health-checks each DC (.202.1/.202.2 /healthz), measures latency, and applies policy (primary DC, override, weights). It drives GoBGP to re-originate 192.168.202.0/32 with next-hop = the chosen DC’s regional VIP at local-pref 500 (active) / 450 (standby); the backbone recursively resolves that next-hop into the DC. The controller is never in the data path — it only tells the backbone which regional VIP to resolve the anycast VIP to.

  • iBGP by necessity — the controllers share the backbone’s AS 65400; eBGP refuses a recursive (non-connected) next-hop (“must be connected”).
  • No loop — an outbound filter on the aggr→DC-border sessions stops the synthesized .202.0 from being flooded back into the EVPN fabric.
  • HA — gobgp-1 down → gobgp-2 (lp450) carries the VIP (verified, zero loss).
  • Steering — set override to DC1/DC2/auto and the VIP follows within a health interval (verified).

Per-client steering: one VIP per client

Anycast is a global decision: one address resolves to one datacenter for everyone at once. To send different clients to different datacenters on purpose, each client gets its own steerable VIP and the controller decides each one on its own.

A single prefix has exactly one best path, so anycast cannot split traffic by source. Rather than guess at the firewall, each client is given its own VIP, advertised from both datacenters and re-originated by the controller exactly like the global VIP. The brain now manages a list of VIPs; each carries its own policy and fails over on its own health signal.

ClientReachesFirewall DNATSteered VIPServing DC

Each per-client VIP is a real load-balanced service in both datacenters, so whichever datacenter the controller points it at can answer. The controller re-originates 192.168.202.3 and 192.168.202.4 at the backbone with next-hop set to the chosen datacenter's regional VIP; the aggregation switches resolve that recursively into the datacenter, and the same outbound filter keeps the synthesized routes from leaking back. The policy is per VIP: external-client-2 holds a static affinity to DC2, while client-1 runs latency mode that prefers DC1 and only rides DC2 on a wide, sustained latency gap. Because both datacenters sit within noise of each other from the controllers, the brain smooths the measured latency (EWMA) and applies a two-threshold hysteresis: client-1 leaves DC1 only when DC2 is faster by more than 1.5 ms, and returns to DC1 once that gap falls back under 1.0 ms. So the route holds steady and only moves on a real, sustained latency shift, not on per-sample noise.

One firewall detail matters: each new VIP's external address has to be added to the internet-to-k8s allow rule, or the translated traffic is dropped even though the DNAT and routing are correct.

Failover drills

Each fault was injected live, measured, and auto-restored. A cross-region ping or an external curl loop ran throughout.

#Fault injectedImpactResult
S1Delete a DC1 app pod0 loss · 140/140surviving replica served; rescheduled
S2Down one border↔aggr link0 loss · 160/160ECMP absorbed it
S3Fail a whole aggr switch~2.8 s · 8.8%reconverged on 2nd aggr (hold-timer bound)
S4Withdraw DC1 anycast VIP0 failed reqsDC1 → DC2 → DC1 (preempted back)
S5Isolate fw-pri from fabric0 failed reqsfw-sec carried north-south

Why S3 isn't sub-second: in EVE-NG the virtual bridge doesn't signal carrier-down, so a downed aggr port doesn't drop the border's link — BGP waits the hold timer. Lowering the aggr↔border hold timer to 3 s roughly halved the outage (from ~8 s). On real hardware, carrier-down or BFD makes this sub-second.

Controller failover drills — the GoBGP anycast controller

Re-run against the new host-based controller, probing the global VIP 10.80.15.50 from external-client-2 every 0.2 s. All three at zero loss.

#Fault injectedImpactResult
C1Kill the active controller (gobgp-1 gobgpd)0 loss · 150/150gobgp-2 (lp450) carried the VIP; DC1 throughout
C2DC1 app failure (dc-demo scaled to 0)0 failed reqsbrain steered .202.0 → DC2, returned on recovery (DC1→DC2→DC1)

Self-healing: the brain reconciles against gobgpd's live RIB, so a controller that loses its BGP daemon re-advertises the VIP automatically on restart (drill C3) — no operator action. Drill C2 is the whole point of the design: a host, not the network, moved the global VIP between DCs on a health signal, at zero loss.

Design decisions

  • One VIP for everything. A single anycast VIP fronts all apps; region selection and cross-region consumption happen server-side, so users only ever see one address.
  • Backbone for east-west, firewall for north-south. Cross-region traffic is routed DC-to-DC over the br-agg backbone (AS 65400, VRF-per-tenant); the firewall is reserved for internet ingress/egress. This removes the firewall's HA3 1500-byte cap from DC-to-DC traffic and unlocks jumbo.
  • Deterministic anycast steering. Equal-length but distinct AS-paths make the two DCs a non-deterministic tie by default; local-pref 200 on DC1 makes it a clean primary/backup with automatic preemption.
  • Fast, safe failure detection. aggr↔border BGP hold timer lowered to 3 s. BFD would give sub-second but bfdd isn't enabled in these images and turning it on means an FRR restart per device — deferred for the POC.
  • Clean config. Retired route-maps, empty policy stanzas, an orphan MLAG SVI, and stale firewall interface references were removed so the running config matches the design with nothing dangling.
  • EVPN Multihoming, not MLAG. The leaf pairs were migrated from MLAG (clag + peerlink) to EVPN Multihoming: host bonds become Ethernet Segments with the old clag mac reused as the es-sys-mac, so the cutover stays host-transparent. No peerlink, no clagd, each leaf its own VTEP; uplink tracking single-homes a host to its peer when a leaf loses all uplinks. The borders, having no dual-homed hosts, became independent VTEPs. The repo keeps both designs (fabric-mlag/ and fabric-evpn-mh/) for side by side comparison.

Leaf redundancy: MLAG vs EVPN Multihoming

The POC ships both designs. Same topology, tenants, anycast controller and firewall; only the leaf and border redundancy mechanism differs. The live fabric now runs Design B.

Pair syncPhysical peerlink + clagdNone; EVPN Type-4 ES routes + DF election
Host bondclag bond (bond mlag id)Ethernet Segment (multihoming segment, es-sys-mac = old clag mac)
VTEPShared anycast VTEP per pairOwn VTEP per leaf

Validated live: every access-leaf Ethernet Segment shows LR (local + remote), zero MLAG left. Redundancy proven by failing a worker leaf: the DC1 demo held 10/10 with hosts single-homed to the surviving leaf. Cumulus VX caveat: the ES bonds converge only after a reboot, and evpn mh redirect-off must be re-applied per boot (VX has no ASIC fast-failover). Configs for both designs: fabric-mlag/ and fabric-evpn-mh/.

Device 8 · dc2-k8s-worker-2/-3 (node) + Kubernetes (L4 to L7)

The node receives the packet for 192.168.202.2:80. kube-proxy / Cilium DNATs it to a backing pod (10.245.1.47:8080 or 10.245.3.240:8080) over the Cilium pod overlay (a second VXLAN, pods 10.245.x). The pod answers HTTP; curl sees "DC2 demo". The reply retraces the path in reverse.

Path: dc1 leaf (L3 lookup, VXLAN encap) → dc1 spine (underlay) → dc1 border (decap, routed) → br-agg (routed backbone, ECMP) → dc2 border (routed, VXLAN encap) → dc2 spine (underlay) → dc2 leaf (decap, L2) → dc2 node → Cilium → pod → app.

Backbone sudo needs the password (key-only). A MAC never crosses DCs; trace a host across DCs by its IP. Two overlays: a cross-DC pod-to-pod packet is encapsulated by Cilium and the fabric. Pod placement is dynamic; the VIP fabric next-hops (device 7) always match the current pod nodes (externalTrafficPolicy Local).

The service workflow, verified live from the ingress leaf

cumulus@leaf-k8s-worker-1:mgmt:~$ sudo ip vrf exec tenant-k8s curl -s -o /dev/null -w "HTTP %{http_code}\n" http://192.168.202.2/
HTTP 200
cumulus@leaf-k8s-worker-1:mgmt:~$ sudo ip vrf exec tenant-k8s ping -c3 192.168.202.2
3 packets transmitted, 0 received, +3 errors, 100% packet loss
cumulus@leaf-k8s-worker-1:mgmt:~$ sudo ip vrf exec tenant-k8s traceroute -n -w1 -q1 -m8 192.168.202.2
 1  192.0.0.8    0.496 ms      # DC1 border (unnumbered hop answers as the FRR dummy address)
 2  10.201.20.1  0.804 ms      # br-agg-sw-1 (backbone)
 3  192.0.0.8    0.885 ms      # DC2 border (unnumbered)
 4  10.168.10.2  1.725 ms      # dc2-k8s-leaf SVI (vlan210)
 5  *                          # the VIP: silent to UDP/ICMP, by design

Three lessons in one screen. curl succeeds while ping fails 100%: a Kubernetes LoadBalancer VIP answers its service port (TCP:80) and nothing else, so ICMP loss to a VIP is not an outage; test TCP. The traceroute empirically walks the exact device path (border, backbone, border, leaf); unnumbered routed hops answer as 192.0.0.8, and the last responding hop is the DC2 leaf SVI before the silent VIP.

The two workflows, step by step

MAC end to end: (1) sudo bridge fdb | grep mac = local on bond1 (2) ip neigh vrf = its IP (3) route vni mac+ip = 3 paths, ESI (4) ES peer shows it local too, show evpn es = LR (5) remote form = extern_learn dst 10.0.0.14 in fdb (6) underlay ECMP + jumbo ping (7) ip -s link counters. Scope: the L2VNI lives only on the pair; cross-DC you trace the IP.

Service end to end: (1) k8s: svc/endpoints/pods (VIP :80, nodePort 32077, pods on w2/w3) (2) Cilium BGP sessions on the leaf (only pod-bearing nodes advertise: ext-Local) (3) ingress leaf: route = both DC1 border VTEPs (4) Type-5 with the fabric-spanning AS-path 65100 65114 65400 65213 65200 65211 65020 + Rmac (5) underlay to the border VTEP (6) border sessions to br-agg, backbone ECMP to both DC2 borders (7) DC2 border re-encaps to VTEP 10.2.0.11 (8) egress leaf: route = the pod nodes, arp-cache + fdb = node MAC on its bond (9) live: curl 200, traceroute walks the path.

The three eBGP-over-eBGP failure classes, verified clean: (1) spine next-hop rewrite: Type-2 next-hop stayed the leaf VTEP 10.0.0.13; (2) route-target loss in transit: RTs + Rmac arrive intact; (3) underlay ECMP asymmetry: every VTEP shows two spine paths and the 9216-byte df-bit ping passes.
